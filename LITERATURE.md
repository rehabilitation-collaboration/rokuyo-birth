# LITERATURE REVIEW
# Rokuyo and Birth Patterns in Japan: An Epidemiological Study
# Last updated: 2026-03-22

---

## Overview

This document records literature reviewed for the study examining the association between rokuyo
(the Japanese six-day auspiciousness calendar) and daily birth counts in Japan. Organized by
thematic cluster: (1) direct rokuyo evidence, (2) Japanese birth pattern literature, (3) Asian
auspicious-day birth literature, and (4) superstition-driven demographic shift literature.

---

## Cluster 1: Direct Rokuyo Evidence

---

## [Hira et al. 1998] Influence of superstition on the date of hospital discharge and medical cost in Japan: retrospective and descriptive study

- **Journal**: BMJ (British Medical Journal), 1998; 317(7174): 1680–1683
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.317.7174.1680 | PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC28746/
- **Data**: Retrospective administrative records of all patients discharged alive from Kyoto University Hospital, April 1, 1992–March 31, 1995. N = 23,677 discharge events. Single-site university hospital (Kyoto).
- **Methods**: Descriptive statistics; mean daily discharge counts by rokuyo day. Kruskal-Wallis test for omnibus comparison across six rokuyo categories; post-hoc Mann-Whitney pairwise tests. Estimated excess cost from extended stays attributed to superstition-driven discharge avoidance.
- **Key results**: Mean discharges/day: Taian 25.8 vs. Butsumetsu 19.3 (ratio ~1.34, p = 0.0001). Hospital stays were longest on Taian (mean 43.1 days) vs. shortest on Butsumetsu (33.3 days, p = 0.0001). Estimated additional cost to the hospital: 7.4 million yen (≈£31,000) over the three-year study period. Authors extrapolated to a nationwide excess of ~4.4 billion yen/year.
- **Limitations**: Single tertiary-care center (not nationally representative); discharge behavior, not birth behavior; no adjustment for confounders (season, diagnosis mix, physician practice); observational design cannot confirm causation; no CI reported for the ratio; extrapolation to nationwide cost is speculative.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: Our study (a) uses nationwide vital statistics across 40 years, (b) focuses on birth events (a stronger proxy for deliberate scheduling than discharge dates), and (c) applies multivariable regression adjusting for day-of-week, public holidays, month, and year trend.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §1 (first empirical evidence that rokuyo influences medical behavior); Discussion §2 (comparison: discharge behavior vs. birth scheduling behavior — same superstition system, different magnitude expected).

## Cluster 2: Japanese Daily Birth Patterns

---

## [Morita et al. 2002] Nationwide description of live Japanese births by day of the week, hour, and location

- **Journal**: Journal of Epidemiology, 2002; 12(4): 330–335
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.2188/jea.12.330 | PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12395874/
- **Data**: All registered live births in Japan in 1998. N = 1,203,147. National vital statistics. Stratified by facility type: hospitals (≥20 beds), clinics (<20 beds or no beds), and maternity homes.
- **Methods**: Descriptive epidemiology. Mean ± SD daily birth counts by day-of-week and hour. Statistical comparison of weekday vs. weekend/holiday daily means. No multivariable regression; no time-series adjustment.
- **Key results**: Mean daily births in hospitals: 1,957 (SD 126) on weekdays vs. 1,433 (SD 100) on weekends/holidays (p not explicitly stated but described as significant). Peak hour: 13:00–14:59 on weekdays. Maternity homes showed no day-of-week variation (mean 34 on Tuesday vs. 32 on Friday; no statistically significant difference). Authors interpreted maternity-home finding as evidence that variations in hospitals/clinics reflect obstetric intervention rather than biological labor rhythm.
- **Limitations**: Single-year cross-section (1998 only); no rokuyo analysis; no multivariable adjustment for seasonality, long-term trend, or holidays; no CI or regression coefficients.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: We analyze 40 years (1979–2018) and include rokuyo as an additional calendar covariate while simultaneously adjusting for day-of-week, holidays, month, and year. Maternity-home data serve as a natural control, replicating Morita et al.'s logic at larger scale.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §2 (establishes that Japanese birth timing is already known to be socially modified); Methods §1 (stratification by birth facility as analytic approach); Discussion §1 (comparison: day-of-week effect size vs. rokuyo effect size).

## [Takahashi M, Nishina M, Ohta A 2014] The effect of determining birthdays by social factors such as maternal preference on the distribution in the number of daily live births

- **Journal**: Japanese Journal of Public Health (日本公衆衛生雑誌), 2014; 61(1): 16–29 [in Japanese]
- **DOI/URL**: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jph/61/1/61_12-081/_article/-char/ja/
- **Data**: National vital statistics (Population Dynamics Statistics), 1981–2010. Daily live births by date and birth facility type (hospitals, clinics, maternity homes). No single sample-size figure reported; full 30-year series.
- **Methods**: Calculated the ratio of daily births to the monthly mean daily births, separately by facility type. Observed average ratios by day-of-week. Examined specific dates (February 29, April 1, March 1, April 2) using standardized deviations. No regression model; descriptive approach.
- **Key results**: Highest mean birth ratio on Tuesday, lowest on Sunday for hospitals and clinics. The ratio gap between weekdays and weekends widened over the study period. Standardized deviations for February 29 and April 1 were extremely negative (−3.0 to −8.3 in hospitals; −5.5 to −13.9 in clinics) up to the mid-1990s, while those for March 1 and April 2 were significantly positive — indicating avoidance of these dates (leap day and school-year entry day). This effect largely disappeared after the mid-1990s, attributed partly to declining fictitious birth registration.
- **Limitations**: Japanese only (no English translation); no multivariable regression; no CI for the day-of-week ratios; no rokuyo analysis; the standardized deviation approach is not directly comparable to regression-based rate ratios.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: We add rokuyo as a covariate to the same data framework and apply negative binomial regression to produce effect estimates with CI. Our time-trend analysis (Phase 4) can test whether rokuyo effects follow a similar secular decline pattern as Takahashi et al. observed for April 1.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §2 (quantitative evidence that Japanese birth dates are influenced by social preferences, from the same data source); Methods §1 (methodological precedent for using daily birth ratios and birth-facility stratification); Discussion §3 (comparison: April 1 avoidance vs. Butsumetsu avoidance as parallel social pressures on birth timing).

## [Takahashi M 2016] The distinctive characteristics of the hourly distribution of live births on specific days in Japan

- **Journal**: Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine, 2016; 21: 501–522
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12199-016-0560-0 | PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5112194/
- **Data**: National vital statistics, 1981–2010. Hourly birth distribution data by specific calendar dates (February 29, March 1, April 1, April 2, year-end holidays) and by facility type.
- **Methods**: Descriptive time-series of hourly and daily birth counts around specific dates. Standardized deviation analysis (z-scores relative to expected daily counts). No formal regression model. Focused on anomalous dates within the broader 30-year dataset of Takahashi 2014.
- **Key results**: Births during 13:00–16:59 were significantly lower on February 29 and April 1 (especially on weekdays). The anomaly was much more pronounced in clinics than hospitals and faded after the 1990s, suggesting both birth certificate fraud (false reporting of date) and scheduled obstetric intervention contributed. On April 2, birthrates were significantly elevated — the reverse of April 1. Hospital births on April 1 showed approximately −20% deviation; clinic births even more negative (−40 to −75% on some dates).
- **Limitations**: Japanese only (English abstract available); descriptive design; no regression with CI; confounding by day-of-week not formally adjusted; sample limited to 1981–2010.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: Our negative binomial regression framework properly adjusts for day-of-week, month, and year, providing formal statistical tests that are absent here.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §2 (evidence of deliberate birth timing manipulation in Japanese obstetric practice, including for social/cultural reasons); Discussion §3 (precedent for a secular decline in culturally driven birth manipulation — relevant to our time-trend analysis).

## [Sassa M et al. 2024] Holiday effect on childbirth: A population-based analysis of 21,869,652 birth records, 1979–2018

- **Journal**: PLOS ONE, 2024; 19(2): e0296403
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0296403 | PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10866518/
- **Data**: Birth certificate microdata from the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (Vital Statistics of Japan). N = 21,869,652 live births, January 1, 1979–December 31, 2018. Includes birthweight, gestational age, and birthday. Grouped into: low birthweight (<2,500 g), preterm (<37 weeks), both, and others.
- **Methods**: Population-based descriptive analysis. Daily birth ratios relative to overall mean by day-of-week and holiday status. Trend analysis by decade. No regression model with explicit covariates; primarily graphical and tabular description.
- **Key results**: Births significantly fewer on weekends and public holidays. Daily birth ratio rises from Monday (lowest among weekdays) to Thursday/Friday (peak), then drops sharply on Saturday–Sunday. The weekly cycle pattern was especially pronounced for high-risk births (low birthweight, preterm). High-risk births concentrated on Thursday–Friday. Effect became more marked over the 40-year period. Authors interpreted the pattern as reflecting medical staff scheduling preferences, particularly for high-risk deliveries.
- **Limitations**: No multivariable regression; no CI for effect estimates; does not include rokuyo or other auspiciousness calendar variables; no analysis of birth facility type; cannot distinguish induction vs. elective cesarean from birth certificate data.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: We apply multivariable negative binomial regression to the same data period with rokuyo added as the primary covariate of interest, while formally adjusting for day-of-week (documented here as a major confounder), month, and year. This study is the most direct methodological predecessor and documents the strongest confounder (day-of-week) we must control.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §2 (largest published analysis of Japanese birth timing; establishes day-of-week effect as the primary known social modulator); Methods §2 (justification for including day-of-week and holiday as covariates); Discussion §1 (effect-size comparison: day-of-week RR vs. rokuyo RR).

## Cluster 3: Asian Auspicious-Day Birth Literature

---

## [Lo JC 2003] Patients' attitudes vs. physicians' determination: implications for cesarean sections

- **Journal**: Social Science & Medicine, 2003; 57(1): 91–96
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0277-9536(02)00301-5 (verified via PubMed PMID:12753818)
- **Data**: Administrative birth records from Taiwan, 1998. N not confirmed in available secondary sources; covers a substantial fraction of annual Taiwan births (~270,000 in 1998). Focused on cesarean section rates by calendar date auspiciousness.
- **Methods**: Comparison of cesarean section rate on auspicious vs. non-auspicious dates. Auspiciousness defined by traditional Taiwanese/Chinese cosmological calendar (distinct from rokuyo). Likely logistic regression or chi-square comparison; precise model specification not available from secondary sources.
- **Key results**: Cesarean section rate was approximately 14% higher on culturally auspicious dates. Authors concluded that maternal preference for auspicious birth dates is a significant non-medical driver of elective cesarean section in Taiwan, independent of physician incentives.
- **Limitations**: Uses Taiwanese Chinese almanac auspiciousness criteria (not rokuyo; conceptually analogous but structurally different); single year (1998); outcome is cesarean mode, not total birth count; does not analyze birth volume shifts; DOI and full methods require direct article access to verify.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: We examine a different calendar system (rokuyo, which is unique to Japan), a different outcome (daily birth count rather than cesarean mode), and a 40-year nationwide dataset rather than a single-year convenience sample.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §3 (cross-cultural precedent that auspiciousness beliefs influence birth scheduling in East Asian populations beyond Japan); Discussion §2 (comparative context: Taiwan cesarean timing driven by choice of lucky day vs. Japan rokuyo birth avoidance/preference).

## [Lin HC, Xirasagar S, Tung YC 2006] Impact of a cultural belief about ghost month on delivery mode in Taiwan

- **Journal**: Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health, 2006; 60(6): 522–526
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.1136/jech.2005.041475 | PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2563930/
- **Data**: Population-based administrative data from Taiwan's national health insurance system. N = 1,750,862 singleton deliveries, 1997–2003. Age-stratified sub-analyses (< 25, 25–34, ≥ 35 years).
- **Methods**: Multivariate ARIMA (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average) modeling of monthly cesarean section rates, 1997–2003. Model selection based on MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error). Final model: ARIMA(3,0,0)₁₂ for pooled sample (MAPE = 1.35%); AR(2) for age-stratified models. Dummy variables for lunar June and July were included as intervention terms. Adjusted for major obstetric complications (previous CS, breech, dystocia, fetal distress).
- **Key results**: CS rates were significantly lower in lunar July (ghost month: 31.32%) vs. June (36.10%) and August (34.94%), p < 0.001. The effect was significant for women aged < 25 and 25–34 but not for women ≥ 35. Authors interpreted the age gradient as evidence that older, higher-risk women are less able to exercise discretion over delivery mode, confirming that the ghost-month effect is driven by discretionary (elective) CS.
- **Limitations**: Monthly, not daily, analysis (temporal resolution insufficient to detect day-level rokuyo-type effects); Taiwan-specific cultural context; ARIMA approach requires sufficient time series length; cannot separate maternal vs. physician preference.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: Our analysis operates at daily resolution (not monthly), capturing the fine-grained six-day rokuyo cycle; Japan vs. Taiwan represents distinct cultural contexts; negative binomial regression is more interpretable than ARIMA for count outcomes with a clear reference category.
- **Citation role**: Methods §2 (key methodological reference for time-series approaches to cultural-belief × birth-timing research; justification for why we chose negative binomial regression over ARIMA — daily resolution, clear categorical rokuyo variable); Discussion §2 (comparison: ghost-month avoidance in Taiwan ~5 percentage points CS rate reduction vs. rokuyo effects in Japan).

## [Huang C, Ma X, Zhang S, Zhao Q 2020] Numerological preferences, timing of births and the long-term effect on schooling

- **Journal**: Journal of Population Economics, 2020; 33(2): 531–554
- **DOI/URL**: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00148-019-00758-1
- **Data**: Administrative birth certificate records from Guangdong Province, China. Sample size not confirmed in available secondary sources; covers a multi-year register of Guangdong births. A second dataset on college attendance used for long-term analysis. Comparison provinces (Jiangxi, Guangxi, Hainan) used as controls.
- **Methods**: Regression analysis of daily birth counts on auspiciousness indicators based on traditional Chinese numerological calendar (auspicious numbers, not rokuyo). Likely Poisson or negative binomial regression for count outcomes (model specification requires direct article access). Difference-in-differences design using neighboring provinces without the same cultural beliefs as controls. Separate analysis for C-section vs. vaginal birth. Long-term outcome: college attendance linked via second dataset.
- **Key results**: ~3.9% excess births on auspicious days; ~1.4% birth avoidance on inauspicious days. C-section rate +2.4% on auspicious days, −1.1% on inauspicious days. Higher male/female birth ratio on auspicious days (sex-selective scheduling). Individuals born on auspicious days more likely to attend college (long-term effect possibly via composition bias — more planned/higher-SES births on selected dates).
- **Limitations**: Guangdong-specific (authors note results may not generalize nationally); Chinese numerological auspiciousness criterion is different from rokuyo; long-term schooling result may reflect parental SES confounding rather than birth-date effect per se.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: Rokuyo is structurally different from Chinese numerological calendars — it is a rotating six-day cycle with no numerological basis — and applies uniformly across all Japanese households regardless of SES. Japan's universal birth registration and our 40-year nationwide dataset provide far greater statistical power than a single-province register.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §3 (most methodologically rigorous Asian study of auspicious-day birth manipulation; establishes that such effects are detectable and quantifiable in administrative data); Methods §2 (comparison reference for effect-size expectations: ~4% excess births on auspicious days suggests rokuyo effects, if present, are likely in the 1–5% range).

## Cluster 4: Superstition-Driven Demographic Shift

---

## [Kaku K & Matsumoto YS 1975] Influence of a folk superstition on fertility of Japanese in California and Hawaii, 1966

- **Journal**: American Journal of Public Health, 1975; 65(2): 170–174
- **DOI/URL**: PubMed ID: 1052742 (companion paper: Kaku K. Annals of Human Biology, 1975; 2(2): 111–115, doi: 10.1080/03014467500000651)
- **Data**: Vital statistics from California and Hawaii Japanese-American communities in 1966 (AJPH paper); companion paper (Ann Hum Biol) uses national Japanese vital statistics 1963–1969 for trend analysis. N for Japan national analysis: all live births and induced abortions reported in vital statistics (approximately 1.8 million births/year in adjacent years). Hinoeuma year (Fire Horse, 丙午): a sexagenary cycle combination occurring every 60 years; superstition holds that women born in this year are dangerous and unsuitable as wives.
- **Methods** (Ann Hum Biol paper, primary quantitative paper): Linear regression on annual birth and abortion rates 1963–1969 to estimate expected 1966 values; deviation of observed from expected used to quantify superstition-attributable births avoided and abortions induced. Comparison with neighboring years using residuals from trend line.
- **Key results**: National Japan: approximately 463,000 fewer live births in 1966 (~25% decline) vs. trend-predicted value. Induced abortions in 1966: 65,000 excess above trend (observed rate 43.1 vs. expected 30.6 per 1,000 births). Total fertility rate dropped from ~2.0–2.1 in adjacent years to 1.58 in 1966. California/Hawaii Japanese: similar but attenuated effect, confirming cultural mechanism over geographic one.
- **Limitations**: Ecological study using aggregate vital statistics; cannot identify individual-level decision-making; Hinoeuma is a once-per-60-year event (highly unusual, not representative of typical year-to-year superstition effects); confounding by secular fertility decline cannot be entirely excluded; data quality for 1960s Japan abortion statistics may be imperfect.
- **Overcomeable by this study**: Rokuyo recurs every ~6 days (not every 60 years), providing annual statistical power and enabling trend analysis. Our study captures routine, continuously operating superstition rather than a one-time shock — a complementary, not competing, type of analysis.
- **Citation role**: Introduction §1 (canonical example that Japanese superstitions can produce demographically detectable birth-count changes; establishes historical precedent for the general mechanism this study extends to rokuyo); Discussion §3 (contrast: once-per-generation catastrophic fertility drop [Hinoeuma] vs. recurring subtle daily modulation [rokuyo] — both reflect the same underlying behavioral mechanism at different scales).

## Citation Map

The following map organizes which papers should be cited at each major location in the manuscript.

### Introduction

**§1 — Opening: Japan's birth timing is socially modifiable**
- Sassa et al. 2024 — largest evidence base; 21M births showing weekday concentration
- Morita et al. 2002 — original nationwide evidence; maternity-home null finding as mechanistic clue
- Takahashi M & Nishina M 2014 — extends to 30-year secular trend; confirms social factors (not biology) drive variation

**§2 — Rokuyo as a candidate social driver**
- Hira et al. 1998 — only prior empirical evidence of rokuyo influencing medical behavior; must cite as the direct precursor
- (Survey data on rokuyo belief prevalence: NEXER×三重平安閣 2023, Zexy, Hanayume — to establish that contemporary belief still exists; cite in the same paragraph)

**§3 — Cross-cultural context: auspicious-day birth manipulation is a documented phenomenon**
- Lo 2003 — Taiwan; auspicious date → +14% cesarean rate
- Lin et al. 2006 — Taiwan; ghost month → −5 pp cesarean rate; strongest methodological model
- Huang et al. 2020 — Guangdong; auspicious days → +3.9% birth count; direct analogue to our RR approach

**§4 — Extreme historical precedent in Japan itself**
- Kaku & Matsumoto 1975 — Hinoeuma 1966; proves Japanese demographic behavior responds to calendar superstitions

**§5 — Gap statement**
- All nine papers above: none has examined rokuyo × birth count. This is the gap.

### Methods

**§1 — Data source and prior use of same data**
- Sassa et al. 2024 — same data source (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare vital statistics microdata); validates data quality and completeness
- Morita et al. 2002 — precedent for stratification by birth facility type (hospital vs. maternity home)
- Takahashi 2014 — precedent for daily birth ratio methodology using the same national vital statistics

**§2 — Choice of negative binomial regression over ARIMA**
- Lin et al. 2006 — ARIMA used for monthly CS rates; our daily count data with a clear categorical exposure (six rokuyo categories) is better served by negative binomial regression with fixed effects. Cite as the comparison approach.
- Huang et al. 2020 — likely also used regression for count outcomes; cite for analogy of method with similar research question

**§3 — Covariate selection rationale**
- Sassa et al. 2024 — day-of-week effect documented; must be included as covariate
- Morita et al. 2002 — hospital vs. maternity-home stratification as confounder control strategy
- Takahashi 2016 — specific dates (April 1, Feb 29) showing that fictitious registration is a confounder mostly pre-1995; justification for sensitivity analysis excluding pre-1995 data

### Discussion

**§1 — Magnitude comparison with known day-of-week effect**
- Sassa et al. 2024 — day-of-week RR (Thursday/Friday peak vs. Sunday trough) as benchmark
- Morita et al. 2002 — weekday vs. weekend birth counts as benchmark

**§2 — Comparison with other East Asian auspicious-day studies**
- Lo 2003 — Taiwan cesarean timing (+14% on auspicious dates); compare our rokuyo RR
- Lin et al. 2006 — Taiwan ghost month (−5 pp CS rate); compare temporal scale and effect size
- Huang et al. 2020 — Guangdong birth count (+3.9% on auspicious days); most direct analogue

**§3 — Japan-specific historical and secular context**
- Kaku & Matsumoto 1975 — Hinoeuma as extreme precedent; argue rokuyo is the "everyday" version of the same mechanism
- Takahashi 2014 / Takahashi 2016 — April 1 avoidance as parallel social pressure; use to frame the discussion that multiple calendar-based social influences coexist
- Hira et al. 1998 — rokuyo previously shown to affect discharge; argue birth scheduling represents a stronger, more deliberate expression of the same belief

**§4 — Limitations**
- Morita et al. 2002 — our maternity-home null finding (if confirmed) replicates their result; use to support mechanistic interpretation (obstetric intervention, not biological rhythm)
- Takahashi 2016 — pre-1995 fictitious registration caveat; link to our sensitivity analysis

**§5 — Public health implications**
- Hira et al. 1998 — prior cost estimate for discharge behavior; frame parallel argument for birth scheduling
- Sassa et al. 2024 — holiday-effect concern for high-risk births; rokuyo-based avoidance of Butsumetsu may similarly concentrate high-risk births on adjacent days
